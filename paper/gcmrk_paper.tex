\section*{Abstract}
Most clustering pipelines run a fixed algorithm and then \emph{measure} the
result with a validity index, so the criterion used to judge a partition is not
the criterion the algorithm optimised. This mismatch is acute in gene
co-expression analysis, where a ``good'' module is defined by a
correlation-based objective that no off-the-shelf clusterer maximises directly.
We present \tool{}, an open-source Python tool that inverts this workflow: it
treats a cluster-validity metric as the optimisation target and evolves a
partition that makes that target as good as it can be, using a self-contained
genetic algorithm that depends only on NumPy and SciPy. \tool{} exposes a single
interface over interchangeable targets---a bespoke correlation-based
log-likelihood for modular data and two cluster-count-penalised variants of it,
plus the silhouette, Davies--Bouldin, Calinski--Harabasz, and Gaussian-mixture
BIC/AIC indices---and supports both a single weighted objective and Pareto
(NSGA-II) optimisation of competing criteria. We benchmark the tool on two
synthetic datasets of graded difficulty, on the classic Iris dataset, and on a
breast-cancer RNA-seq dataset. On correlated modular data the correlation
log-likelihood recovers the planted modules with high adjusted Rand index where
Euclidean k-means fails, and a penalised correlation criterion selects the
correct number of modules when it is unknown; on geometric data the same engine
faithfully optimises geometric indices; and on real expression data it recovers
coherent modules whose eigengenes track tumour-versus-normal status. \tool{}
gives practitioners direct, criterion-faithful control over what ``good
clustering'' means for their data.

\section*{Author summary}
When biologists group genes by how similarly they are expressed, they usually
run a standard clustering method and then score the result with a separate
quality measure. The method, however, was never trying to do well on that
measure---it optimises its own internal objective. We built a tool, \tool{},
that removes this gap: the user picks the quality measure they actually care
about, and the tool searches directly for the grouping that scores best on it.
The search is performed by a genetic algorithm, a population-based optimiser
that mixes and mutates candidate groupings over many generations. \tool{}
includes a purpose-built score for ``modules'' of co-expressed genes, as well as
several widely used geometric scores, and it can balance two competing scores at
once to choose how many groups the data support. We show on synthetic data with
a known answer, on a textbook dataset, and on real expression data that the tool
recovers the intended structure and lets researchers make explicit, and
optimise for, their own definition of a good cluster.

\section*{Introduction}
Clustering is a foundational step in computational biology, from grouping
co-expressed genes into putative regulatory modules~\cite{Eisen1998,Wolfe2005}
to organising cells, samples, or conditions. The dominant practice is
two-staged: a fixed algorithm such as k-means~\cite{MacQueen1967} or
hierarchical clustering~\cite{Eisen1998} produces a partition, and a
cluster-validity index---the silhouette~\cite{Rousseeuw1987}, the
Davies--Bouldin index~\cite{DaviesBouldin1979}, the Calinski--Harabasz
criterion~\cite{CalinskiHarabasz1974}, or an information criterion such as
BIC~\cite{Schwarz1978}---is then used to \emph{evaluate} that partition, often
to choose the number of clusters. The objective the algorithm optimises (for
k-means, within-cluster sum of squares) and the objective by which the result is
ultimately judged are therefore distinct. When the two disagree, the reported
partition is optimal for neither the user's stated criterion nor, necessarily,
the underlying biology.

This gap is particularly consequential in gene co-expression analysis. There,
the notion of a good module is intrinsically correlational: genes in a module
should be strongly co-expressed across conditions, regardless of their absolute
expression levels or Euclidean geometry. Tools such as WGCNA~\cite{Langfelder2008},
Clust~\cite{AbuJamous2018}, and UNCLES~\cite{AbuJamous2015} encode this intuition
through correlation networks or consensus procedures, and comparative studies
show that module-detection performance depends strongly on how the objective is
framed~\cite{Saelens2018}. Yet the correlation-based quality of the final
modules is still, in most pipelines, assessed only after the clustering
algorithm has finished optimising a different, geometric objective.

We argue for inverting the workflow: make the validity metric itself the
optimisation target. Concretely, the user specifies the scalar criterion that
defines a good partition for their problem, and a search procedure evolves a
partition to optimise that criterion directly. This is attractive whenever the
criterion of interest is (i) not what standard algorithms optimise, (ii)
non-differentiable or combinatorial, or (iii) most naturally expressed as a
trade-off between competing concerns---fit versus parsimony, compactness versus
separation. Direct optimisation of clustering criteria has a long history in
evolutionary computation~\cite{Maulik2000,Handl2007}, but it is rarely packaged
as a practical, dependency-light tool aimed at the co-expression use case and
built around a correlation objective.

Here we present \tool{} (Genetic Clustering by Metric---Madrigal-Roca and
Kelly), an open-source Python package that clusters data by directly optimising a
chosen cluster-validity metric with a genetic algorithm (GA). \tool{} makes
three contributions. First, it provides a bespoke correlation-based
log-likelihood objective tailored to modular, co-expression-like data, together
with two cluster-count-penalised variants that select the number of modules
within correlation space, alongside standard geometric/probabilistic indices, all
behind one interface so that the criterion is an explicit, swappable choice rather
than a hidden default. Second, it supports both a single weighted objective and
full multi-objective (NSGA-II~\cite{Deb2002}) optimisation, so that competing
criteria---most usefully a goodness-of-fit term traded against a parsimony
term---can be explored as a Pareto front. Third, the implementation is
deliberately minimal: it depends only on NumPy~\cite{Harris2020} and
SciPy~\cite{Virtanen2020}, with internal k-means++~\cite{ArthurVassilvitskii2007}
and correlation-distance hierarchical seeders, and so installs and runs anywhere
those libraries are available. We evaluate \tool{} on two synthetic datasets of
contrasting difficulty, on the classic Iris benchmark~\cite{Fisher1936}, and on a
breast-cancer RNA-seq dataset, and we characterise both its strengths and the
absence of built-in parsimony in the raw correlation objective, together with the
remedies the tool provides.

\section*{Materials and methods}

\subsection*{Overview and problem statement}
\tool{} clusters $n$ \emph{elements} (the rows of a data matrix
$\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}$; in co-expression analysis, $n$ genes
measured across $d$ samples) by searching the space of partitions for one that
optimises a user-chosen validity metric. A partition is represented as an
integer label vector $\boldsymbol{\ell}\in\{0,1,\dots,K\}^{n}$, where
$\ell_i = c$ assigns element $i$ to cluster $c$ and the optional label $0$ marks
an element as unassigned. The user fixes an upper bound $g_{\max}$ on the number
of clusters. Each candidate partition $\boldsymbol{\ell}$ is scored by one or
more metric functions $m(\boldsymbol{\ell})\in\mathbb{R}$; the GA evolves a
population of partitions to optimise these scores in their natural direction
(maximise or minimise).

\subsection*{Performance-target metrics}
\tool{} treats every metric as an interchangeable ``performance target''.
Table~\ref{tab:metrics} lists the built-in targets, the input each consumes, and
its optimisation direction. They fall into two families: correlation-based
objectives designed for modular data, and standard geometric/probabilistic
indices.

\paragraph*{Correlation-based log-likelihood.}
The bespoke objective at the heart of the tool rewards partitions whose
within-cluster elements are mutually correlated. Let
$\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$ be the Pearson correlation matrix between
elements (rows). For a cluster $s$ of size $n_s$, define the aggregate
within-cluster absolute correlation
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cs}
C_s = \sum_{i\in s}\sum_{j\in s} \lvert R_{ij}\rvert ,
\end{equation}
which includes the unit diagonal and is therefore bounded in
$[\,n_s,\; n_s^{2}\,]$: it attains $n_s$ when the off-diagonal correlations
vanish and $n_s^{2}$ when the cluster is perfectly correlated. The score is the
sum over non-singleton clusters
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:loglik}
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\ell}) = \frac{1}{2}\sum_{s\,:\,n_s>1}
\left[\, \log\!\frac{n_s}{C_s}
   + (n_s-1)\,\log\!\frac{n_s^{2}-n_s}{\,n_s^{2}-C_s\,} \,\right],
\end{equation}
which is to be maximised. Each summand is a monotone function of the cluster's
internal coherence: as $C_s \to n_s^{2}$ (a perfectly correlated cluster) the
second term diverges to $+\infty$, the best possible fit; as $C_s \to n_s$ (an
incoherent cluster) the summand approaches zero. Singletons carry no
within-cluster correlation information and contribute nothing. To keep the score
finite and the optimiser stable, a perfectly correlated cluster
($C_s \ge n_s^{2}$) is awarded a large finite reward rather than $+\infty$.

A deliberate property of Eq~\eqref{eq:loglik} is that it measures only
goodness-of-fit and contains \emph{no parsimony penalty}: subdividing a coherent
cluster into smaller, still-coherent pieces does not in general lower
$\mathcal{L}$, so optimising it alone under a loose $g_{\max}$ can over-segment.
We quantify the effect in the Results and address it next.

\paragraph*{Penalised correlation criteria.}
To let a single objective both fit and select the number of modules, we add an
information-criterion penalty to $\mathcal{L}$ that stays entirely within
correlation space. Treating each non-singleton cluster as contributing one
within-cluster correlation parameter, a partition with $K$ such clusters over
$n$ assigned elements is scored
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:loglikic}
\mathrm{IC}(\boldsymbol{\ell}) = -2\,\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\ell}) + \lambda K ,
\end{equation}
to be minimised, with $\lambda = \log n$ giving a BIC-style criterion
(\texttt{loglik\_bic}) and $\lambda = 2$ an AIC-style one (\texttt{loglik\_aic}).
This is distinct from the geometric \texttt{bic}/\texttt{aic} targets, which
assume Euclidean-Gaussian clusters: those are at odds with the
absolute-correlation objective whenever modules contain anti-correlated members,
which is precisely the regime in which \texttt{loglik} is the right criterion.
Because the penalty lives on the same scale as $\mathcal{L}$, optimising
Eq~\eqref{eq:loglikic} directly under a loose $g_{\max}$ recovers a parsimonious
partition without the number of clusters being specified.

\paragraph*{Geometric and probabilistic indices.}
For data whose clusters are defined geometrically, \tool{} provides four
standard indices computed on $\mathbf{X}$: the mean silhouette
coefficient~\cite{Rousseeuw1987} (maximise; range $[-1,1]$), the Davies--Bouldin
index~\cite{DaviesBouldin1979} (minimise), the Calinski--Harabasz
variance-ratio criterion~\cite{CalinskiHarabasz1974} (maximise), and the
Bayesian and Akaike information criteria~\cite{Schwarz1978,Akaike1974} under a
spherical-Gaussian-per-cluster model with pooled variance (both minimise). The
BIC/AIC log-likelihood follows the x-means formulation~\cite{Pelyhe2000}; for a
partition with $k$ clusters in $d$ dimensions the model has
$p = kd + (k-1) + 1$ free parameters (centroids, mixture weights, and one pooled
variance), and BIC $=-2\log\hat{L} + p\log n$, AIC $=-2\log\hat{L} + 2p$. Unlike
the correlation objective, BIC and AIC reward parsimony explicitly and so can be
used for model selection on geometrically compact data.

\subsection*{Data preparation}
Input is a numeric matrix (CSV/TSV or array) with rows as elements and columns
as features; header rows and index columns are auto-detected. By default the
data are standardised to zero mean and unit variance. For the correlation
objective, standardisation is applied per row (per element/gene), which is the
regime under which the Pearson correlation between rows is meaningful; for the
geometric indices, per-feature standardisation is the appropriate choice. The
element-by-element correlation matrix $\mathbf{R}$ is computed once and reused
across all evaluations of $\mathcal{L}$.

\subsection*{Genetic algorithm}
\tool{} evolves a population of partitions with a self-contained GA
(Fig~\ref{fig:convergence}). The components are:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Initialisation.} The initial population is seeded with three kinds
of partition across candidate cluster counts $k = 2,\dots,g_{\max}$: k-means++
partitions (a small internal NumPy k-means with multiple restarts);
\emph{correlation-aware} partitions obtained by agglomerative (average- and
complete-linkage) hierarchical clustering of the correlation distance
$1-\lvert\mathbf{R}\rvert$, the classical co-expression clustering strategy; and
random partitions for diversity. User-supplied seeds (e.g.\ the output of WGCNA
or Clust) can also be injected. The correlation-aware seeds are important for the
\texttt{loglik} objective: Euclidean k-means splits strongly anti-correlated
elements that are coherent under the absolute-correlation criterion, whereas the
correlation-distance seeds already respect the structure the GA is asked to
optimise.
\item \textbf{Evaluation.} Each partition is scored on the chosen target(s); a
cache keyed on the label vector avoids recomputing identical partitions.
\item \textbf{Selection and variation.} In \emph{weighted} mode the targets are
combined into a single fitness through direction-aware weights after a bounded
squashing transform that places metrics of different scales on a comparable
footing, and elitist tournament selection is applied. In \emph{nsga2} mode the
targets are treated as competing objectives and NSGA-II~\cite{Deb2002}
fast non-dominated sorting with crowding distance is used. Variation is two-point
crossover on label vectors plus a reassignment mutation that moves a small
fraction of elements to random clusters.
\item \textbf{Repair.} After every operator a partition is relabelled to
consecutive integers and constrained to have between $2$ and $g_{\max}$ clusters,
so that all validity metrics remain well defined (degenerate single-cluster or
empty-cluster partitions are repaired).
\end{enumerate}

For multi-objective runs \tool{} returns the full Pareto front and, as a single
representative partition, the knee point that maximises the sum of min--max
normalised objectives across the front. Unless stated otherwise, all experiments
use a population of 200 evolved for 120 generations, two k-means restarts per
candidate $k$, crossover probability 0.8, mutation probability 0.2 with per-gene
reassignment probability 0.05, tournament size 3, and elitism 5, with a fixed
random seed for reproducibility.

\subsection*{Choosing the number of clusters}
Because $\mathcal{L}$ (Eq~\eqref{eq:loglik}) has no parsimony term, \tool{}
offers three routes to the right number of clusters. (i) \emph{Constrain}
$g_{\max}$ to the expected number of modules. (ii) \emph{Optimise a penalised
correlation criterion} (\texttt{loglik\_aic} or \texttt{loglik\_bic},
Eq~\eqref{eq:loglikic}) as a single objective under a loose $g_{\max}$; this
selects $K$ within correlation space and is our recommended default when $K$ is
unknown. Equivalently, one can scan $g_{\max}$ with \texttt{loglik} and pick the
$K$ that minimises Eq~\eqref{eq:loglikic}. (iii) \emph{Explore an explicit
trade-off} by optimising \texttt{loglik} jointly with a parsimony target under
NSGA-II and reading the knee of the Pareto front; pairing with the geometric
\texttt{bic} is informative only when the modules are also geometrically compact,
whereas the penalised correlation criteria avoid that assumption.

\subsection*{Benchmark datasets and evaluation}
We evaluate on three datasets with known ground truth and one empirical
expression dataset (a second is planned).

\emph{Synthetic-Easy} and \emph{Synthetic-Hard} are generated by \tool{}'s
modular simulator: each module is driven by a shared latent factor (with random
$\pm 1$ loadings, so within-module genes are strongly correlated in absolute
value while genes in different modules are not), with per-entry Gaussian noise
added and rows finally standardised. Synthetic-Easy has three equal modules
(\EasyGenes{} genes across \EasySamples{} samples) at low noise, giving strong
separation (mean within-module $\lvert r\rvert = \EasyWithinR{}$ versus
between-module $\EasyBetweenR{}$). Synthetic-Hard has five unequal modules
(15, 20, 25, 30, 10 genes; \HardGenes{} genes across \HardSamples{} samples) at
higher noise, giving weak separation (within $\HardWithinR{}$ versus between
$\HardBetweenR{}$).

\emph{Iris}~\cite{Fisher1936} is the classic \IrisSamples{}-sample,
\IrisFeatures{}-feature, 3-class benchmark; it is low-dimensional and geometric
rather than correlational, and we use it to show that the same engine, driven by
geometric targets, faithfully optimises those indices on data outside the
co-expression regime.

\emph{Empirical data.} We apply \tool{} to GSE183947~\cite{Wang2021}, a breast
cancer RNA-seq dataset of \EmpSamples{} samples (30 tumours and 30
patient-matched adjacent-normal tissues) with \EmpFullGenes{} genes
quantified as FPKM. After $\log_2$ transformation we cluster the \EmpNtop{} most
variable genes into co-expression modules, and assess each module both by its
internal correlation coherence and by whether its eigengene (the leading
principal component of the module's expression across samples) distinguishes
tumour from normal tissue (Welch $t$-test), linking the recovered modules to the
clinical phenotype. A second empirical dataset will be added.

We compare \tool{} against a k-means baseline (10 restarts, true $k$) on the same
standardised data. Agreement with ground truth is measured by the adjusted Rand
index (ARI)~\cite{HubertArabie1985} and by clustering accuracy under the optimal
label permutation (Hungarian assignment). All code to reproduce the experiments,
figures, and tables is provided with the package.

\section*{Results}

\subsection*{Recovering correlated modules on synthetic data}
On Synthetic-Easy, \tool{} driven by the correlation log-likelihood (with
$g_{\max}$ set to the true count) recovers the planted three-module structure
perfectly (ARI $=\EasyLoglikARI{}$), whereas the k-means baseline---given the
same true $k$---fails badly (ARI $=\EasyKmeansARI{}$). The gap is diagnostic:
because module loadings carry random signs, each module contains strongly
anti-correlated genes that are coherent under the absolute-correlation objective
but lie far apart in Euclidean space, so k-means splits them. The GA converges
within the first few dozen generations (Fig~\ref{fig:convergence}). On
Synthetic-Hard, where weak separation and higher noise make the problem
substantially harder, \tool{} still recovers the five modules well
(ARI $=\HardLoglikARI{}$) while k-means remains poor (ARI $=\HardKmeansARI{}$).
The correlation-aware hierarchical seeding (Methods) is important here: it
supplies the GA with starting partitions that already respect the
absolute-correlation structure, which a Euclidean seeder cannot.

\subsection*{Selecting the number of clusters without knowing it}
When only \texttt{loglik} is optimised under a loose $g_{\max}$, the correlation
objective can over-segment, since Eq~\eqref{eq:loglik} carries no parsimony term.
With correlation-aware seeding the effect is mild but real: on Synthetic-Easy the
loglik optimum already coincides with the true partition ($k=\EasyLooseK{}$),
whereas on Synthetic-Hard a loose bound yields $k=\HardLooseK{}$ (true 5;
Table~\ref{tab:benchmark}). The penalised correlation criteria
(Eq~\eqref{eq:loglikic}) pin down the count within a single objective: optimising
\texttt{loglik\_aic} under the same loose $g_{\max}$ returns exactly
$k=\HardAicK{}$ on Synthetic-Hard at ARI $=\HardAicARI{}$ (and $k=\EasyAicK{}$ at
ARI $=\EasyAicARI{}$ on Synthetic-Easy), recovering the true number of modules
without it being specified. The heavier BIC-style penalty (\texttt{loglik\_bic})
is more conservative: exact on the well-separated easy data ($k=\EasyBicK{}$) but
merging the weakest modules on the noisy hard data ($k=\HardBicK{}$), the
expected behaviour of a stronger complexity penalty when separation is marginal.
The trade-off is also visible by scanning $g_{\max}$ (Fig~\ref{fig:modelsel}):
\texttt{loglik} rises and then plateaus with $k$ while \texttt{loglik\_aic} turns
over near the true number of modules.

For users who prefer to inspect an explicit trade-off rather than commit to a
single penalised criterion, the multi-objective mode optimises \texttt{loglik}
against a parsimony target under NSGA-II and returns the full Pareto front
(Fig~\ref{fig:pareto}). We note that pairing \texttt{loglik} with the
\emph{geometric} BIC is less effective on these data, because BIC's
Euclidean-Gaussian assumption conflicts with the absolute-correlation objective
when modules contain anti-correlated members---which is exactly why the penalised
\emph{correlation} criteria are preferable for module selection.

\subsection*{Generality on geometric data: Iris}
On Iris, a geometric rather than correlational problem, the same GA engine driven
by geometric targets faithfully reaches each index's optimum. Both
\texttt{silhouette} and \texttt{calinski\_harabasz} select $k=\IrisSilK{}$ even
when permitted more clusters (ARI $=\IrisSilARI{}$ against the three botanical
species), which is the well-documented behaviour of internal validity indices on
Iris: they merge the two overlapping species (\emph{versicolor} and
\emph{virginica}) because that two-group split is geometrically cleaner
(Fig~\ref{fig:iris}). A k-means run \emph{told} to use $k=3$ recovers the species
at ARI $=\IrisKmeansARI{}$. The contrast is the intended lesson, not a deficiency:
\tool{} optimises whichever criterion it is given, so on Iris it returns the
partition those indices genuinely prefer. This confirms that the tool is not
specific to co-expression---the contribution is the criterion-faithful
optimisation framework, with the correlation objective as the specialised target
for modular data.

correlation criterion \texttt{loglik\_aic}, \tool{} resolves \EmpKmodules{}

\section*{Discussion}
\tool{} reframes clustering as direct optimisation of the validity criterion the
user cares about, closing the gap between the objective an algorithm optimises
and the objective by which its output is judged. The benefit is most concrete in
co-expression analysis, where the correlation-based log-likelihood
(Eq~\eqref{eq:loglik}) encodes ``module = mutually correlated genes'' as the
optimisation target rather than as a post-hoc check. Our synthetic experiments
show that, when the number of modules is known or bounded, this objective
recovers planted modules at least as well as a strong k-means baseline, and that
on genuinely correlational data it is the more appropriate criterion.

The raw correlation log-likelihood rewards fit without penalising complexity, so
unconstrained optimisation over-segments. Rather than hide this, we address it
directly with penalised correlation criteria (Eq~\eqref{eq:loglikic}) that add a
cluster-count penalty without leaving correlation space; optimised as a single
objective under a loose bound, \texttt{loglik\_aic} recovered both the number of
modules and the partition on our synthetic data, and selected coherent,
phenotype-associated modules on real data. This is more robust than pairing the
correlation objective with a geometric information criterion under NSGA-II,
because the Euclidean-Gaussian assumption of the latter conflicts with the
absolute-correlation objective when modules contain anti-correlated members. A
second ingredient that proved important was correlation-aware seeding: starting
the population from hierarchical clustering of the correlation distance, rather
than from Euclidean k-means, markedly improved recovery on the harder synthetic
data. Remaining directions include alternative co-expression objectives (e.g.\
topological overlap), recombination operators that respect cluster boundaries,
and scaling to genome-wide matrices, where the $\mathcal{O}(n^2)$ correlation
matrix and per-evaluation cost become the bottleneck and gene pre-filtering (as
used here) is currently required.

Methodologically, \tool{} sits in the tradition of GA-based and multi-objective
clustering~\cite{Maulik2000,Handl2007} but is distinguished by its
co-expression-oriented correlation objective, its single swappable-metric
interface, and a deliberately minimal dependency footprint that makes it easy to
deploy. By making the definition of a good cluster an explicit, optimisable
choice, the tool gives practitioners control over a decision that conventional
pipelines leave implicit.

\section*{Conclusion}
We introduced \tool{}, a dependency-light Python tool that clusters data by
directly optimising a user-chosen cluster-validity metric with a genetic
algorithm, including a bespoke correlation-based log-likelihood for modular
co-expression data and standard geometric/probabilistic indices, in both
weighted and Pareto modes. Across synthetic data of graded difficulty, the
classic Iris benchmark, and empirical expression data, \tool{} recovers intended
structure and lets users make explicit, and optimise for, their own definition of
cluster quality. The software, documentation, and full reproduction scripts are
openly available.
